Why Soundline

Every child has a soundline.

Middle school is three years. They go fast. Your child might be doing fine on paper and quietly checked out. Or bored, waiting for everyone else. Or working hard, and nobody's noticed.

Before sonar, sailors dropped a sounding line over the side: a rope marked in fathoms, weighted with lead. It told them how deep the water was, and a dab of tallow in the weight brought up a piece of the bottom, so they knew what was underneath, too. Only then did they set a course.

That's where we start with your child. Not only where they are, skill by skill, but what sparks them and how deep it runs. Then we set a direction together: the next skill, the next project, the next thing worth showing their town. Nobody moves on until they've got it, and nobody waits once they have.

Ten to twelve in a grade.

Nobody's stuck in it. Students work by skill across the school, and on Fridays the whole school builds one thing together. Small enough there's nowhere to hide, big enough for a real seminar.

One advisor, three years.

Every student has the same guide as their advisor from the day they start, in a group of ten to twelve.

Grouped by skill, not age.

A sixth grader and an eighth grader can work the same skill at the same table. Each moves on when it's mastered.

Three grades, one building.

And a grade added every year through twelfth, starting with ninth. The older students are out in town on apprenticeships.

The questions that come up first.

Isn't ten to twelve too small?

It's three grades in one building, and it adds a grade every year through twelfth. Your child's working group changes by subject, the whole school is together on Fridays, and every adult knows your child by name.

What about high school?

We add ninth grade in our second year, then a grade a year through twelfth. New York's diploma now rests on six things a graduate can do, and your child makes, defends or shows every one of them here, every six weeks.

Is Soundline approved by New York?

New York doesn't license private schools. You register with the state, and the district reviews that what you teach is equivalent. We do all of it, then start accreditation once we're open.

Will our district bus my child?

Most likely. Districts provide transportation to private schools up to fifteen miles away. You request it from your own district by April 1, and we'll send you the request already filled in.

A day here

Real work, finished, and shown to someone.

Mornings are practice.

Math, reading, and writing at each student's own pace, with a guide in the room. Nobody waits for the class to catch up, and nobody gets left behind it. Two hours, done well, and done.

Afternoons are for making.

History, literature, and science are taught by a person with a book, never by an app. Studio runs in six-week projects with a real audience: a robot, a documentary, a zine the school press publishes, a song the ensemble performs.

Big work gets defended out loud. No slides, no screen. A student, their work, and good questions.

Same rhythm every week, so the work can be different.

MondayLaunch. 8:45. One question, one goal, one plan for the week.
TuesdayLab, then studio. Ninety minutes, hands on, written up before studio. Then everyone makes something.
WednesdaySeminar. A novel, a question, a paragraph. Every seminar ends in writing.
ThursdayApprenticeships. Grades 7 and 8 work alongside someone who does it for a living. Younger students build their Quest.
FridayAll In, then Showcase. The whole school takes on one thing together. At 2:00, someone presents and something is performed. Usually loud. Families welcome.

A Tuesday

One seventh grader. One day.

September, the first six-week cycle. No name, because it isn't any one child's day yet.

8:30The door. The same face every morning. Bag on the hook, phone in the box, a word about the weekend.
8:45Launch. Advisory, fifteen minutes. Today's goal: clear the proportions gate, draft the lab question.
9:00Math, at their own gate. The gate opens when they can show it to a guide, not when the unit ends. Across the table, a sixth grader is on a gate this student cleared in week one.
9:50Reading and writing. Today's text: how ice carved the Sound, because the lab needs it. Two paragraphs, one an argument.
10:30Math seminar. The whole school, problems out loud. Someone proves why the shortcut works; this student finds the case where it doesn't.
11:00Lunch and the walk. Outside, every day, whatever the weather. Most of what a guide learns about a student, they learn here.
11:40Lab: the sounder. A sensor on a line that reads the depth of a tank. Today is calibration, and the graph bends where it shouldn't. Defended Friday.
1:15Studio: the Quest. The client is the children's librarian: an exhibit on how sound travels through water. Forty dollars, a plan, and a speaker that won't stay sealed. A guide is in the room and does not fix it.
2:15The ensemble. Everyone plays something; nobody has to be good yet. The bass part is this student's.
3:00Home, or not yet. A lesson at 3:15, then the homework desk until 5:30. The Quest stays at school.

And how they got there.

New York just decided a diploma should mean six things, not a set of test scores. We built the school on those six. Every six weeks, your child adds to a folder of proof: made, defended, grown. By eighth grade it's a portfolio, and it goes with them wherever they go next.

How you'll know it's working.

We measure growth three times a year and publish it.

NWEA MAP, three times a year. We publish whole-school growth, counting everyone. No child's score is public; yours comes home.
