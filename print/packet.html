Why Soundline

Every child has a soundline.

Middle school is three years. They go fast. Your child might be doing fine on paper and quietly checked out. Or bored, waiting for everyone else. Or working hard, and nobody's noticed.

Before sonar, sailors dropped a sounding line over the side: a rope marked in fathoms, weighted with lead. It told them how deep the water was, and a dab of tallow in the weight brought up a piece of the bottom, so they knew what was underneath, too. Only then did they set a course.

That's where we start with your child. Not only where they are, skill by skill, but what sparks them and how deep it runs. Then we set a direction together: the next skill, the next project, the next thing worth showing their town. Nobody moves on until they've got it, and nobody waits once they have.

A day here

Students do real work, finish it, and show it to someone.

Mornings are practice.

Math, reading, and writing at each student's own pace, with a guide in the room. Nobody waits for the class to catch up, and nobody gets left behind it. Two hours, done well, and done.

Afternoons are for making.

History, literature, and science are taught by a person with a book, never by an app. Studio runs in six-week projects with a real audience: a video game, a robot, a sculpture, a short documentary, a zine the school press publishes, a song the ensemble performs.

Big work gets defended out loud.

No slides, no screen. A student, their work, and good questions.

A Tuesday

One seventh grader. One day. Hour by hour.

September, the first six-week cycle. The Tuesday we'll run, followed through one student's day. No name, because it isn't any one child's day yet.

8:30Arrival
The door.

The same face every morning. Bag on the hook, phone in the box, a word about the weekend.

8:45Launch
Advisory, fifteen minutes.

Ten to twelve students, one guide for three years. Today's goal: clear the proportions gate, draft the lab question.

9:00Practice · 50 min
Math, at their own gate.

The gate opens when they can show it to a guide, not when the unit ends. Across the table, a sixth grader is on a gate this student cleared in week one; beside them, an eighth grader is on one they'll reach by winter.

9:50Practice · 40 min
Reading and writing.

Today's text: how ice carved the Sound, because the lab needs it. Two paragraphs, one an argument.

10:30Seminar · 30 min
Math seminar.

The whole school, problems out loud. Someone proves why the shortcut works; this student finds the case where it doesn't. The fourth try lands.

11:00Outside · 40 min
Lunch and the walk.

Outside, every day, whatever the weather. Most of what a guide learns about a student, they learn here.

11:40Lab · 95 min
The sounder.

A sensor on a line that reads the depth of a tank: the sounding line, rebuilt. Today is calibration, and the graph bends where it shouldn't. Defended Friday.

1:15Studio · 60 min
The Quest.

Week three of six. The client is the children's librarian: an exhibit on how sound travels through water. The group has a plan, forty dollars, and a speaker that won't stay sealed. A guide is in the room and does not fix it. From the second cycle, Tuesday afternoons are apprenticeships for grades 7 and 8.

2:15Ensemble · 45 min
The ensemble.

Everyone plays something; nobody has to be good yet. The bass part is this student's.

3:00Extended Day
Home, or not yet.

A lesson at 3:15, then the homework desk until 5:30. Or the carline. The quest stays at school.

Why it works

None of this is new. Putting it in one building is.

Mornings

You don't move on until you've got it.

Mastery before moving on beats the calendar, in over a hundred studies. Struggling students gain most.

Afternoons

Real projects raise real scores.

In randomized trials, long projects beat the usual way on state tests.

Seminar

Writing it down is how it sticks.

Writing about it, and saying it out loud, is how it sticks.

Advisory

Being known changes how hard a student works.

One close teacher relationship is among the strongest predictors of effort, more so with age. Hence one advisor for three years.

Questions families ask

The ones that come up first.

How many students will there be?

Ten to twelve in a grade — and nobody's stuck in their grade. Students work by skill across the school, and on Fridays the whole school builds one thing together. Small enough there's nowhere to hide, big enough for a real seminar.

Isn't that too small?

It's three grades in one building, and it adds a grade every year through twelfth. Your child's working group changes by subject, the whole school is together on Fridays, and the older students are out in town on apprenticeships. And every adult in the building knows your child by name.

What does it cost?

We publish tuition on Thursday, November 12, the morning after the information night, and founding enrollment opens with it. It's paid monthly over twelve months, and founding families get a rate that holds through twelfth grade — not just through eighth. The current figures are always at soundlineschool.com/cost.html.

What about high school?

We add ninth grade in our second year, then a grade every year through twelfth. New York has moved to a diploma built on evidence of six things a graduate can do, and every one of them is something your child makes, defends, or shows here, every six weeks. That folder of work goes with them wherever they go next.

Is Soundline approved by New York?

New York doesn't license private schools. You register with the state, and the district reviews that what you teach is equivalent. We do all of it, then start accreditation once we're open.

We homeschool. Can we join?

Yes. The Collaborative is three days a week, Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, with the same guides, rooms, and studio. You keep your homeschool plan, and we hand you the course descriptions and hours logs to go with it.

Will our district bus my child?

Most likely. New York districts provide transportation to private schools up to fifteen miles away. You request it from your own district by April 1, and we'll send you the request already filled in.

My child has an IEP. What happens to services?

Your child can still receive services from the public school district. You request them from the district where the school is located by June 1, and we'll help you do it.

Can my child still play school sports?

New York requires students to be enrolled in the district to play on district teams, so town and club teams are the way to go — and they're where most students this age already play. We have PE in the day, and Extended Day runs until 5:30.

How much screen time is there?

Less than you'd think. Morning practice is hands-on, at a table with a guide; the only screen in the morning belongs to the guide. Seminar, lab, and the defense happen without screens. AI is taught as a subject, never used as a shortcut.

Where will the school be?

In Miller Place. We announce the building the day the lease is signed.

Something we didn't answer? Write to hello@soundlineschool.com. If we don't know, we'll find out and tell you.
